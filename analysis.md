SUMMARY
The research paper of Google File System(or GFS) written by Ghemawat et al talks about GFS. It is a scalable distributed file system designed to support large-scale data-intensive applications at Google. It provides high throughput, fault tolerance, and efficient handling of massive files that are stored across thousands of inexpensive, unreliable machines. The paper presents the motivation, architecture and performance of the GFS, emphasizing pragmatic design choices over the traditional file system guarantees.
The basic architecture consists of three type of nodes - client, master and chunkservers. GFS employs a single master server that stores the metadata, and coordinates the chunkservers, who store the file data in chunks of 64mb size each. These chunks are in turn replicated across multiple machines(ideally, atleast 3). Clients interact directly with chunkservers for data transfer, reducing the master’s load. A lease-based mechanism ensures consistent mutation ordering across replicas. The system offers strong fault tolerance through replication,checksumming and re-replication on failures.By relaxing strict POSIX semantics and focusing on high aggregate throughput instead of low-latency access, GFS effectively supports Google’s workloads such as large-scale web indexing and log processing. The paper concludes with experimental results demonstrating that GFS achieves high performance and reliability on clusters built from commodity hardware, influencing many later distributed storage systems like the Hadoop Distributed File System (HDFS).

PROBLEM DESCRIPTION
Before GFS, traditional file systems were not designed for the massive scale, fault rates, and workload patterns that Google faced. Conventional systems assumed expensive, reliable hardware and small files accessed by few clients. However, Google’s applications needed to store and process terabytes of data across thousands of unreliable commodity machines, with workloads dominated by large sequential reads and appends. Existing distributed systems failed to provide both scalability and reliability at low cost. GFS was designed to solve this gap by providing a fault-tolerant, high-throughput distributed storage that could sustain frequent hardware failures while efficiently managing very large data sets.

CORE SOLUTION DESIGN
The GFS architecture is based on three main components: a single master, multiple chunkservers, and clients. Files are divided into large fixed-size chunks (typically 64 MB), each identified by a unique handle and replicated (usually three copies) across chunkservers for fault tolerance. The master manages the file system namespace, metadata, and the mapping from files to chunks. It maintains this information persistently through an operation log and checkpoints. Importantly, the master is not on the data path, and instead, clients communicate directly with chunkservers for reading and writing data, which minimizes bottlenecks. For consistency and mutation ordering, GFS uses a lease mechanism. When a client wants to write, the master grants one replica a temporary lease to act as the primary, responsible for serializing all mutations to that chunk. The primary coordinates updates among secondary replicas to ensure consistent data. This design eliminates the need for heavy distributed consensus protocols while maintaining efficient ordering guarantees. Record append is a key feature, optimized for concurrent, large-scale log writing. Multiple clients can append simultaneously, and GFS guarantees that each record is appended atomically at least once, even if failures occur. Fault tolerance is built into every level. Chunkservers send regular heartbeats to the master. If a failure is detected, the master automatically re-replicates chunks from healthy replicas. Checksums detect data corruption, and clients verify integrity on reads. GFS relaxes strict POSIX semantics, trading consistency for performance and availability. Applications built on top of GFS, such as MapReduce, are designed to handle duplicates or minor inconsistencies. Overall, GFS’s design philosophy centers on simplicity, scalability, and fault-tolerant performance tailored for large distributed systems using unreliable hardware.

STRENGTHS AND WEAKNESSES
Strengths - 

Scalability : GFS efficiently scales to thousands of machines. The single-master design, combined with large chunk sizes and client-side caching of metadata, ensures that metadata operations remain lightweight.
Fault Tolerance : Replication, Re-replication on failures, and checksum-based corruption detection provide high data recovarability and failure management without human intervention.
High throughput : By focusing on large sequential reads and appends, GFS achieves excellent aggregate throughput. Clients communicate directly with chunkservers, avoiding bottlenecks at the master.
Simplicity and Managaebility : The architecture is fairly simple and using one master ensures metadata consistencty and coordination among chunkservers.
Adaptation to workload patterns : GFS’s relaxed consistency model and record append operation align perfectly with Google’s workloads, such as web crawling and log-based data analysis. These design choices maximize efficiency while maintaining acceptable consistency for application-level requirements.
These are some of the reasons it has become a blueprint for modern distributed systems. 
Weaknesses -
Single point of failure and potential bottleneck : Although fault-tolerant through replication, the master remains a central coordination point. Under metadata-heavy workloads (many small files or frequent namespace changes), it may become a performance bottleneck.
Poor support for small files : Large chunk sizes and metadata caching favor huge files but degrade efficiency when dealing with many small files. Metadata overhead per file becomes high relative to data stored.
Limited suitability for latency-sensitive workloads : GFS is optimized for throughput, not for low-latency random reads or writes, making it less effective for real-time applications.
Complex recovery scenarios: While automated, master recovery and re-replication can take time in large clusters, temporarily reducing performance.

PERSONAL REFLECTION
GFS is the first real world application of distributed systems I have seen that is used on a large scale. It taught me how in practical scenarios, theoretical ideals are balanced. I learned that engineering trade-offs—such as prioritizing throughput and fault tolerance over strict consistency—can lead to highly effective real-world systems. The idea of assuming failure as inevitable, rather than exceptional, fundamentally changed how I think about system reliability. The concept of leases for lightweight coordination and record append for concurrent workloads were particularly elegant solutions.
The paper also deepened my understanding of related concepts like replication, fault detection, and also introduced new concepts like checksumming, and metadata management in distributed environments. It closely connects with ideas from Leslie Lamport’s paper on ordering events in distributed systems (I read a little bit of its summary), which provides the theoretical basis for understanding GFS’s consistency management. Additonally, GFS also influenced later technologies like HDFS and MapReduce - which I learned about a little from the MIT lecture, and hence I can see how large-scale data processing is performed commodity hardware.
If I were designing GFS myself, I would experiment with decentralizing some of the master’s responsibilities to reduce the single point of coordination. For example, implementing multiple masters or a sharded metadata service could improve scalability for workloads involving many small files or frequent namespace operations. I would also enhance support for small files through techniques such as metadata caching layers or bundling many small files into larger storage blocks. Additionally, I would try incorporating a hybrid consistency model, where strict consistency is maintained for critical data- such as financial, but relaxed semantics are used for bulk workloads.
